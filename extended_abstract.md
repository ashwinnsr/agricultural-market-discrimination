# Market Access in Rural India: An Investigation of Agricultural Output Pricing

**Extended Abstract & Project Proposal**  
**Author:** Ashwin  
**Data Sources:** National Sample Survey (NSS) 77th Round (2019–20) & Foundation for Agrarian Studies (PARI) Village Surveys  

---

## 1. Purpose or Objective
This study investigates how social identity (caste) and economic position (class/landholding) interact to shape agricultural output market access and price realization among farm households in rural India. Combining a nationally representative empirical diagnostic with village studies, the project asks whether observed pricing differentials stem from identity-based market penalties, structural channel exclusion, scale economies, or localized inter-linked market dependencies.

## 2. Motivation or Rationale
Policy interventions in Indian agricultural marketing — APMC reforms, electronic trading platforms (e-NAM), procurement expansion — seek to improve price realization through better infrastructure and lower transaction costs. But agrarian markets in developing economies remain embedded within local social hierarchies and informal institutional arrangements. Thorat and Newman (2010) describe "selective inclusion" as a form of discrimination in which excluded groups are not denied market access outright but are admitted on differential terms. In output markets, Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) smallholders — who already face entry barriers in land and credit markets — may experience such selective inclusion at the point of sale. Whether these disadvantages spill over into pricing, and whether formal marketing channels mitigate or reproduce them, remains an open empirical question.

## 3. Theoretical Framing
How we conceptualize agricultural output markets shapes how reform is designed. We frame the market experience of marginalized cultivators through a **"double squeeze"** mechanism operating across both input and output markets. On the input side, marginalized social groups face capital constraints, lower credit access, and lower paid-out input intensity (*the first squeeze*). On the output side, price realization is mediated by a household's structural position in the agrarian class structure and social hierarchy via information asymmetry (MSP awareness), informal debt tie-in sales, and channel exclusion (*the second squeeze*). A lower price realized by a marginalized farmer may thus reflect direct identity-based discrimination, statistical discrimination by intermediaries under information asymmetry, or the implicit cost of inter-linked credit–labour–marketing arrangements with local traders. Disentangling these channels is necessary before any one of them can be treated as the operative mechanism.

## 4. Methods and Databases
The paper uses unit-level transaction data from the National Sample Survey (NSS) 77th Round Situation Assessment Survey of Agricultural Households (2019, $n \approx 74,500$ transaction records across 52,634 unique households). We estimate high-dimensional fixed-effects regressions controlling for crop-by-district unobservables, sales volume, monthly per capita expenditure (MPCE), and landholding class, with standard errors clustered at the primary sampling unit (FSU) level. Supplementary methods include channel-conditional Linear Probability Models (LPMs) for market-channel access, quantile regressions across the price distribution, Blinder–Oaxaca decompositions of the raw caste price gap, and explicit regression models for mechanism channels (MSP awareness, informal indebtedness, and paid-out input expenses).

**Operationalization of Agrarian Class:**  
- **Stage 1 (NSS 77 Diagnostic):** Class is operationalized using three complementary proxies: (i) continuous land possessed ($\log \text{total\_land}$ in acres), (ii) five discrete landholding size classes derived from hectares possessed—*Marginal* ($<1.0\text{ ha}$, including zero-land cultivators), *Small* ($1.0–2.0\text{ ha}$), *Semi-Medium* ($2.0–4.0\text{ ha}$), *Medium* ($4.0–10.0\text{ ha}$), and *Large* ($\ge 10.0\text{ ha}$)—and (iii) household Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure ($\text{MPCE}$) to control for consumption liquidity and wealth independent of land scale.  
- **Stage 2 (PARI Micro-Investigation):** Moving beyond observational survey asset proxies, Stage 2 operationalizes class structurally through landlord-tenant tenancy contracts (sharecropping terms), informal debt inter-locking with traders/landlords, and hiring-in/hiring-out labour exploitation relations.

To probe the localized mechanisms that a national survey cannot resolve, the second stage draws on village census data from the Project on Agrarian Relations in India (PARI), conducted by the Foundation for Agrarian Studies, in selected villages in Uttar Pradesh and Tripura chosen for contrasting tenurial and institutional contexts.

## 5. Results (Preliminary Findings)
Fixed-effects models controlling for crop-by-district unobservables, sales volume, and MPCE show a small and only marginally significant SC price penalty of roughly −1.0% to −1.1% in log price relative to General Caste farmers; the coefficient is not robust to every specification and loses significance once district fixed effects are replaced with state fixed effects. ST farmers show no penalty in the baseline specification, but a significant −4.1% penalty emerges under state-level (rather than district-level) geographic controls — a pattern consistent with ST cultivation being geographically concentrated in lower-price districts, and one that needs to be disentangled before it can be read as a price penalty proper. SC farmers are also significantly less likely to sell through APMC mandis, and report significantly lower satisfaction with sale outcomes and a higher incidence of price-related dissatisfaction than General Caste farmers, even though realized prices in these channels do not differ sharply by caste.

A national caste $\times$ landholding interaction model ($M7$) proved uninformative due to severe cell sparsity—large-landholding SC households number only seven observations nationally ($n=7$), rendering national estimates of caste–class interaction statistically unreliable and necessitating village-level census data.

**Empirical Mechanisms (The Double Squeeze):**
1. *Information Asymmetry:* SC households are 8.3 percentage points less likely to be aware of Minimum Support Prices ($p < 0.001$), and ST households 15.0 percentage points less likely ($p < 0.001$), controlling for land, MPCE, and location.
2. *Informal Credit Exposure & Tied Sales:* SC households are 6.7 percentage points more likely to hold debt from informal moneylenders or local traders ($p = 0.006$), exposing them to price-suppressing inter-linked contracts.
3. *Input Squeeze:* Controlling for crop and district fixed effects, SC cultivators spend 10.7% less on paid-out inputs ($p = 0.003$) and ST cultivators 64.5% less ($p < 0.001$), establishing clear credit and capital constraints on the production side prior to output marketing.

Blinder–Oaxaca decompositions are highly sensitive to the inclusion of land controls: for ST households, the unexplained share of the gap swings from roughly +19% of the total gap (land included) to −4% (land excluded), and for SC households from roughly +31% to −370%, with the SC total gap itself close to zero (−0.0097 log points, or −0.96%) and therefore statistically fragile as a base for decomposition. This instability is itself a finding — it demonstrates that a nationally representative but non-essential survey cannot on its own separate a caste effect from the land-class structure it is correlated with, and motivates the shift to village-level data where tenurial and credit relationships can be observed directly rather than proxied.

## 6. Implications and Planned Directives
These preliminary results suggest that national-level diagnostics can identify where and for whom price disparities are largest, but cannot on their own adjudicate between the candidate mechanisms — market power, scale economies, geographic sorting, or direct discrimination — that produce them. The project's next phase uses PARI village census data to examine inter-linked credit–output contracts, tenurial relationships, and informal trader networks directly, exploiting the fact that these villages record the specific contractual terms linking credit, tenancy, and output sale that a national survey instrument cannot capture. Contrasting land tenancy arrangements in Uttar Pradesh with statutory tribal land protections under Autonomous District Councils in Tripura will allow the study to examine how local class relations and institutional safeguards mediate market access for India's marginalized agrarian producers, and to assess whether the national-level patterns identified here reflect the mechanisms the village studies are designed to trace.
